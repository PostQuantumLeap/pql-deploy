# The FINMA Guidance 05/2026 report

This page explains what the FINMA Guidance 05/2026 report in Post Quantum Leap measures, which findings can fail, and which parts of the guidance it does not assess. Read it before you quote one of its figures to a supervisor.

**Who:** every signed-in role (Reader, PKI Operator, System Administrator) can view it.

## Where to find it

Open **Compliance** and find the FINMA Guidance 05/2026 card. The card shows the headline figure and a short explanation of what the report measures. **Open report** opens the per-endpoint breakdown, and **Excel** exports it. See [Compliance](../09-compliance.md) for how every report is laid out.

## What it is

FINMA Guidance 05/2026 *Quantum computing* was published on 9 July 2026. It is an *Aufsichtsmitteilung*, a supervisory notice. It is not a *Rundschreiben* (a circular), and it creates no new obligation.

The correct name is **FINMA Guidance 05/2026**. "FINMA RS 05/2026" is wrong.

If you assemble evidence for your supervisor, get this distinction right first. The card, its edition line and its Excel export all state it, so nobody has to take it from this page.

## What can fail

Everything in §3 of the guidance recommends. Nothing there obliges. So no rule sourced from §3 can fail in this report.

Only three rules can fail. All three are classical defects, present today:

| Rule that can fail | Why it can fail |
|---|---|
| SSL, TLS 1.0 or TLS 1.1 offered | Rests on §1 of the guidance. §1 states that the binding governance and risk-management requirements of financial market law already cover quantum risk, and that FINMA expects supervised institutions to address it in a timely manner. |
| NULL, anonymous, export, RC4, DES or 3DES cipher accepted | Same anchor. The configuration is broken classically, today, whatever quantum computing does. |
| SHA-1 or MD5 certificate signature | Same anchor. SHA-1 collision attacks are practical and public. |

Because of this, the headline reads **Endpoints free of TLS defects**, not "endpoints conforming". The wording is deliberate. The report does not produce a FINMA compliance rate, and cannot.

## What only warns, and why

Every quantum-vulnerability rule in this report is a warning. None can fail.

Each of them rests on §3, and §3 recommends. Nothing in it obliges, so nothing sourced from it can fail. This covers the certificate rules, the key-exchange rule and the criticality rule described below.

## Why the certificate rules fire on nearly everything

No public CA issues ML-DSA certificates yet. So "certificate chain carries quantum-vulnerable public keys" is true of nearly every endpoint you own, and stays true until that changes.

This is not a defect list. The near-100% figure is the inventory statement that §3.2 asks you to produce.

The figure that will move over the next year is the key-exchange one. The large CDNs and browsers are turning hybrid ML-KEM on by default. That is why certificates and key exchange are separate rules rather than one.

## Hybrid and pure ML-KEM

The report counts hybrid and pure ML-KEM the same.

§3.3 reports that various organisations prefer hybrid in the short to medium term. It gives the counter-argument (added complexity) in the next sentence, and leaves the choice to your own risk analysis. FINMA takes no position, so the report encodes none.

## What is not assessed

Two of FINMA's five recommendation areas are not assessed at all, and a third only in the negative. The report says so. It does not score them green.

| Area | Why the report cannot assess it |
|---|---|
| §3.1 Strategy and roadmap | Asks for a board-adopted strategy, an implementation plan with milestones and target dates, and a PQC roadmap by mid-2027. Each is a signed document. "Percentage of the estate migrated" is no substitute: an institution with a board-adopted roadmap and nothing migrated satisfies §3.1 completely, while one at 40% hybrid because a CDN changed a default has no plan at all. |
| §3.4 Crypto-agility | A property of your architecture and your procurement contracts. A scan proves one negative only: an endpoint that offers no TLS 1.3 cannot negotiate ML-KEM by any configuration change, because those groups exist in no earlier version. It can never prove the positive. An agile stack and a hard-coded one produce byte-identical handshakes. |
| §3.5 External service providers | A scan cannot tell an outsourced endpoint from an in-house one. Your own data centre and your cloud provider present the same handshake. The substance of §3.5 is contractual, and its central point, that responsibility for an outsourced function stays with you, is exactly where a false green would do the most harm. |

Two further limits apply before you quote any figure:

- **§3.2 is covered in its algorithm half only.** The scan sees what one listening socket negotiates. It sees nothing about data at rest, key management, HSMs, code-signing keys or DLT nodes.
- **No scanner can measure the completeness of its own inventory.** "Conforming: 100%" over a partially discovered estate is true and worthless at the same time. Coverage is the number §3.2 most wants, and the one this report cannot produce.

## The criticality label rule

§3.3 asks you to prioritise data that needs long-term confidentiality. Nothing in a handshake says whether the bytes are a public marketing page or thirty-year custody records. So this rule reads the **Criticality** governance attribute instead (see [Governance attributes](../12-governance.md)).

The rule's text names the label. It does not claim to have found "critical data". On an estate with no criticality labels, the rule fires on nothing.

## The 90-day refresh finding

"Record not refreshed for over 90 days" is a defect in the inventory, not in the endpoint. You clear it with a rescan, not a fix.

The 90 days is PQL's own figure. FINMA states no interval. It was chosen so that a quarterly rescan satisfies it.
